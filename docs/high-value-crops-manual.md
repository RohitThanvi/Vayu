# Growing high-value crops: a starter manual

*Generated 2026-11-07 from Vayu's sourced knowledge base. Organic and cultural methods only.*

## Read this first

This manual is for someone trying a new crop, including someone farming for the first time. It tells you what each crop needs, what to do when your conditions are not ideal,
and how to grow and look after it. It is built from published sources (ICAR institutes, state agricultural universities, CSIR, FAO, Vikaspedia, and similar) and from general
agronomic knowledge. **Every line says which it is.** A line marked *general knowledge* has not been verified against a source here.

It is an advisory aid, not a guarantee of yield or income, and not a replacement for your local Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK). Some crops here fail often or cost a lot to set up; the
risk and capital ratings are meant to be taken seriously.

### Before you spend money (general knowledge)

1. **Visit your nearest KVK** or state agricultural university and tell them the crop you are considering. Ask for their local advice and for planting material they trust.
2. **Get your soil tested** and check your water source and its reliability through the whole season.
3. **Find your buyer first.** Know who will buy, in what form, at what grade, and how you will get it there before you plant at scale.
4. **Start small.** Plant a fraction of what you could. Learn the crop, then expand from what actually worked on your land.
5. **Keep records:** dates, inputs, weather events, problems, harvest weights and sale prices. They are how you learn and how you get credit later.

### About chemicals

This guide gives organic and cultural methods only. It does not name or dose pesticides, fungicides or chemical fertilisers; for those, follow your nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university and the label of any registered product.

### How "can it grow here?" is decided

| Tier | Meaning |
|---|---|
| 1. Open-field suitable | Nothing in the climate and soil checks is outside the crop's cited range |
| 2. Open-field with help | Gaps exist but each has a field-level fix (irrigation, drainage, mulch, shade, soil amendment) |
| 3. Controlled environment | The crop is grown in a built space (a mushroom shed, a chilled saffron room), not by matching the open climate |
| 4. Not realistic here | A blocking gap (for example hard frost for a frost-sensitive crop) with no field-level fix |

Monthly average climate cannot show frost nights, hot dry winds or heat waves. Where that matters the report adds a caution; **check your own lowest and highest temperatures.**

## Dragon fruit (Kamalam): how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 3/5 · capital needed: high · risk: medium · first return: about 1-2 years from cuttings (general)

*Hardy and low in pest problems, but needs poles/trellis up front and the market price varies a lot with fruit size and pulp colour.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **frost** (fixable): Freezing injures the plant; it is not suitable for temperate or frost-prone areas. Where winters are only mildly cool, protect young plants; do not plant where hard frost is normal. [S2]
- **heat** (fixable): Very high temperature for long periods (above about 40 C) harms growth, flowering and fruit. Use a shade net over young plants and fruit, and mulch the base. [S2]
- **dry** (fixable): Low rainfall is workable (commercial crops reported from about 400-500 mm) with drip irrigation and mulch. [S2,S3]
- **wet** (fixable): Heavy rain and waterlogging cause stem rot. Plant on raised beds or mounds with drainage; avoid low-lying land. [S1,S2]
- **ph** (fixable): Bring pH toward 5.5-7.0 with compost and farmyard manure; get a soil test before adding any amendment. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Planting material

- Propagate from stem cuttings of a healthy mother plant; ready-to-plant rooted cuttings take about 4-5 months in polybags (soil : FYM : sand). [S2]
- Buy only from a reputable nursery or an ICAR/KVK source; poor planting material spreads disease. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Expert guidance is available from ICAR-IIHR, ICAR-NIASM Baramati and ICAR-CIARI Port Blair. [S1,S3]

### Land preparation and planting

- Full sun, well-drained soil. Sandy loam to loam rich in organic matter is best; all soil types can work if drained. [S2]
- Install a post (about 1.5-2 m high) with a ring on top for each planting spot; the plant climbs. Common spacing is 3 m x 3 m (single post); 2 m x 2 m is also used. [S2]
- Apply 10-15 kg farmyard manure per plant at planting. [S1]
- Keep water from standing around the base of the stem. [S2]

### Irrigation

- Keep soil moist but never soggy; this is a cactus and excess moisture rots the stem. [S4]
- Drip irrigation with mulch saves water and keeps the base dry. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Nutrition (organic)

- Rely on organic manure: farmyard manure, compost, neem cake; poultry manure can supplement. ICAR-IIHR notes it can be grown organically. [S1,S2]
- Re-apply compost or manure every few months and mulch around the plant. [S4]

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Stem rot and spots are the main problems and are linked to heavy rain and over-watering; prevent with drainage, clean tools and removing and destroying infected stems. [S1]
- Bag fruits to protect them from sunburn, birds and insects. [S1]
- A weed mat or mulch reduces weeds and conserves moisture. [S1]
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Walk the field weekly: check stem bases for soft or discoloured patches, yellowing, sunburn marks, and new flower buds. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Note flowering flushes (typically in 3-5 staggered flushes, June to November) and harvest 30-35 days after flowering. [S5]

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Fruits are ready about 30-35 days after flowering; weight is typically 200-700 g depending on management. [S5]
- Harvest season in India is mainly June to October. [S4]
- Yellow varieties can be stored 28-30 days at 10 C. [S6]

### Risks and costs

- Initial cost is mainly poles/trellis and planting material, so capital is needed before the first fruit. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Market price is highly variable and depends on fruit size and pulp colour. [S5]
- India's area is growing, so check local demand and a buyer before planting a large area. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

## Moringa (drumstick): how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 2/5 · capital needed: low · risk: low · first return: first pods about 6-8 months after planting for seed types such as PKM-1 (general; TNAU notes flowering from ~90-100 days)

*Drought-tolerant, low capital, grows in poor soil and many climates. Income depends on a reliable buyer for pods or leaf.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **frost** (fixable): It survives only light frost. In frost-prone areas protect young trees (cover, plant in a sheltered spot) or choose another crop. [S4]
- **dry** (fixable): Drought-tolerant, but flowering and yield improve with irrigation; use drip or basin irrigation in dry areas. [S2,S5]
- **wet** (fixable): It will not survive prolonged flooding. On heavy rain or poor drainage, plant on mounds and keep water away from the trunk. [S6,S5]
- **ph** (fixable): Tolerates a wide pH (5.0-9.0 reported) but best near neutral; add compost and get a soil test. [S6]

### Planting material

- Annual types such as PKM-1 and PKM-2 (from TNAU) grow from seed; seed rate about 500 g per hectare. Sow two seeds per pit, or raise seedlings in polybags and transplant after 35-40 days. [S1]
- Get seed from a TNAU, ICAR or KVK source so you plant a known variety. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Land preparation and planting

- Planting season is July to October. Dig pits 45 x 45 x 45 cm at 2.0-2.5 m spacing and mix 15 kg compost or farmyard manure with topsoil in each pit. [S1]
- Closer spacing (for example 1.2 m x 1.2 m for PKM-2) is used for high-density plantings. [S1]
- Pinch the main shoot when seedlings are about 60-80 cm; repeat pinching during the first 3 months to get a bushy, easy-to-harvest tree. [S1,S7,S3]

### Irrigation

- Irrigate before sowing and on the 3rd day, then every 10-15 days depending on soil type. [S8]
- Avoid excess moisture; root rot follows waterlogging. [S3]

### Nutrition (organic)

- Pit mix of farmyard manure or compost with a little neem cake; repeat compost each year. [S3]
- Mulch the base to conserve moisture. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Main pests: leaf-eating caterpillars, bud worm, termite, aphid, leaf miner and fruit fly; root rot from excess moisture. [S3]
- Hang fermented tomato fruit traps (about 25 per hectare) for pod fly. [S8]
- Scout weekly, pick off caterpillars by hand, keep the field clean, and use neem-based preparations only as a spot treatment. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- An official AESA-based IPM package for drumstick exists from NIPHM; ask your KVK for it. [S9]
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Look under leaves and at growing tips weekly for caterpillars, aphids and leaf miner damage; check the trunk base for rot after rain. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Record flowering and pod set dates. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- In a semi-arid western India trial, PKM-2 gave 44.3 kg of fruit per tree and PKM-1 36.7 kg. [S10]
- A KVK page gives an example of about Rs 1.25 lakh net income per acre per year at Rs 10/kg; treat this as one illustrative example with unstated assumptions, not a forecast. [S3]
- Pods are best sold fresh; line up a buyer or a nearby mandi before the first harvest. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Risks and costs

- Pod prices swing with the season and glut periods; a few trees for home use plus a small commercial block is a lower-risk start. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Rainfall that is continuous gives near-continuous yield; dry spells can be bridged with irrigation. [S11]

## Oyster mushroom: how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 2/5 · capital needed: low · risk: medium · first return: about 3-4 weeks from spawning (spawn run 15-20 days, then pinheads)

*Needs no land and little capital, which suits a first try. Hygiene and finding a steady buyer matter most; fresh mushrooms spoil fast.*

### What you would need to build

A controlled setup: a shaded, well-ventilated room or shed; no land needed. Plains: roughly October to March when temperature is 20-30 C; hills: March to October (ICAR NEH). Outside that window you need cooling or must wait.

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **heat** (fixable): Above about 30 C, growth and quality fall. Grow in the cooler months, or use a thick-walled shed with wet gunny cloth on the walls and floor sprinkling to bring the temperature down. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- **dry air** (fixable): Keep room humidity at 70-90 percent by sprinkling water on walls and floor (not on the bags). [S1,S3]

### Planting material

- Buy tested spawn from ICAR-DMR Solan, an ICAR institute or agricultural university, or a reliable private spawn lab. About one 250 g packet of spawn is enough for 2 bags of 60 x 45 cm. [S2,S4]
- Use fresh spawn and use it quickly; old or contaminated spawn is the most common cause of failure. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Land preparation and planting

- Substrate: paddy or wheat straw, chopped. Paddy straw reached a biological efficiency of about 77 percent at ICAR-CIARI; wheat straw can do better. [S2,S5]
- Pasteurise or sterilise the chopped straw with heat (hot water or steam) before filling bags; do not use chemical dips. Ask your KVK for the exact time and temperature they recommend. [S2]
- Fill transparent bags (about 60 x 45 cm), layer spawn with straw, tie, make a few small holes, and hang or rack the bags in a dark, clean, well-aerated room. [S1,S2]

### Irrigation

- No watering of the bags until pinheads form; keep the room humid by sprinkling walls and floor. [S1]
- Once fruiting starts, light misting keeps the surface moist; avoid soaking the bags. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Nutrition (organic)

- The straw is the food; no added fertiliser is needed. Used (spent) substrate can be composted afterwards. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- During spawn run keep 20-28 C and about 85 percent humidity; remove any bag showing contamination immediately. [S4]
- Neem leaf extract has been tested as an organic biocontrol in oyster mushroom beds at an ICAR centre, with higher yield; ask your KVK how to use it. [S6]
- Keep the room clean, wash hands and tools, keep flies out with fine net on windows, and do not store old bags or straw nearby. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Check daily: temperature, humidity, white mycelium spreading, any green, black or orange patches (contamination), and pinhead formation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Spawn run takes 15-20 days; flushes come about 10 days apart, 3-4 flushes per crop. [S1,S4]

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Harvest each flush when the caps are fully formed but before the edges curl; twist and pull gently. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- An ICAR-CIARI example: about Rs 59,000 investment for 20 quintals of straw over 12 months, with gross returns around Rs 85,000 from 5 crops. This is a regional illustration; prices and costs change. [S2]
- Sell fresh the same day where possible; mushrooms can also be dried. [S4]

### Risks and costs

- Contamination can wipe out a batch; start with a few bags and learn before scaling. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Fresh mushrooms are perishable, so secure a nearby buyer or market first. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

## Lemongrass (citral oil): how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 2/5 · capital needed: medium · risk: medium · first return: first cut about 90 days after planting; best yields in years 2-3 (cited)

*Hardy, grows on poor and degraded land and animals leave it alone. The catch is the market: income comes from selling herb to a distillery or distilling oil yourself.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **dry** (fixable): Lower rainfall can be grown with supplemental irrigation (TNAU). Expect a lower oil yield without it, and plan drip or basin irrigation. [S2]
- **wet** (fixable): Waterlogged soil should be avoided. Plant on ridges and keep drainage open. [S1,S4]
- **ph** (fixable): Aim for about pH 5.0-7.5 (6.0 is called suitable by TNAU). Avoid calcareous soil; add compost. [S1,S2]
- **frost** (fixable): It is a warm-climate grass; protect young clumps from frost or choose a warmer site. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Planting material

- Plant root slips (2-3 tillers each, 25-30 cm long, old roots clipped) from healthy, well-grown clumps. Slips give better oil quality than seed. [S1,S5]
- Recommended varieties include Krishna, Pragati, Nima, CKP-25 and OD-19; Krishna suits irrigated and rainfed land. CSIR-CIMAP (Lucknow) and its Aroma Mission supply slips and advice. [S4,S5,S6]

### Land preparation and planting

- Plant June-July (last week of May to first week of June in some regions). Open ridges 60 cm apart and plant 2-3 slips per hill. [S1,S4]
- Spacing 45 x 45 cm in plains, or 60 x 45 cm on slopes; 60 x 60 cm was used in an ICAR-IISWC trial. [S4,S7]
- Mix farmyard manure or compost (about 10 tonnes per hectare) into the soil before planting. [S1]

### Irrigation

- Supplemental irrigation in drier areas and in dry spells. [S1]
- Irrigated crops give more oil than rainfed crops. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Nutrition (organic)

- Farmyard manure or compost at planting and each year. Spent distilled grass applied as mulch (about 3 tonnes per hectare) controls weeds and keeps soil moist. [S1,S4]

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Keep the crop weed-free in the early stage; mulch with spent grass. [S4]
- Lemongrass has relatively few serious pests; scout for leaf spots in humid weather and remove badly affected clumps. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Check clump health and weed pressure monthly; record each cutting date and the herb weight. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Oil recovery rises and falls with season and cutting age, so keep records to find your best cutting time. [S4]

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- First harvest about 90 days after planting, then every 75-90 days. Cut 10-15 cm above ground, ideally early morning. [S2]
- Herb yield reported: about 215 quintals per hectare in year 1, 360 in years 2-3, 215 in year 4; oil roughly 150 / 250 / 250 / 150 kg per hectare at 0.7 percent recovery. Recoveries between 0.2 and 1.0 percent are reported. [S4,S2]
- Wilt the herb 6-8 hours and distil soon after harvest; steam distillation takes about 3-4 hours in a field unit. [S6]

### Risks and costs

- A distillation unit is the main capital cost; many growers sell herb to an existing distiller or join an aroma cluster. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Oil prices change with demand; confirm a buyer and current rates before you plant. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

## Saffron: how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 5/5 · capital needed: high · risk: high · first return: first flowers about 3-4 months after planting corms (flowering around Oct-Nov for July planting)

*Not a first-farm crop. It needs engineered climate control, imported corms and technical skill, and published indoor protocols are still being standardised. Visit or train with a working unit before spending money.*

### What you would need to build

A controlled setup: an insulated room with chillers, humidifiers and grow lights (aeroponic racks or trays) that recreates Kashmir's cold, humid cycle. Corms are planted around July; flowering is induced by a cold spell and comes around October-November.

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **not kashmir climate** (blocking): Open-field saffron depends on a cold winter and a dry summer dormancy. Outside the Kashmir-type climate the realistic route is a controlled indoor room, which is an engineering and capital decision, not a farming one. [S1,S2,S3]
- **indoor climate control** (fixable): Indoor growers report a warm phase (about 15-20 C) for sprouting and a cold flowering phase (single digits to about 17 C). Reported figures differ between sources, so use a trained supplier's schedule, not a blog's. [S4,S6]

### Planting material

- Buy graded corms from Pampore, Kashmir (SKUAST-Kashmir or a reputable grower). Corms of about 7-8 g and above are used; bigger corms flower more. [S1,S3,S7]
- Treat the corm bill as the main upfront cost: one reported indoor grower paid about Rs 8 lakh for a tonne of corms and Rs 6.5 lakh for the room. One personal example, not a typical cost. [S5]

### Land preparation and planting

- Open field (Kashmir): plant 15-30 July, at 15-20 cm depth, on raised or well-drained beds; rows 20-25 cm apart. Delayed planting lowers first-year flowers. [S3,S7]
- Indoor: a thermally insulated room with chillers, humidifiers, light and CO2 control; clean, restricted-entry space. [S1,S2,S6]
- There is no single proven indoor protocol yet; a state horticultural university described its own project as a pilot that will produce standard operating procedures. [S1]

### Irrigation

- Open field: pre- and post-flowering irrigation raises flowering and corm yield; rain or irrigation in August-September helps early flowering. Keep drainage open to avoid corm rot. [S8,S3]
- Indoor aeroponic: nutrient mist delivered on a timed schedule; humidity about 60-70 percent is reported. [S1,S6]
- Stop watering during summer dormancy. [S4]

### Nutrition (organic)

- Organic matter (well-rotted farmyard manure or compost) in beds for open field; indoors, follow your supplier's nutrient plan. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Corm rot (fungal) is the commonest cause of failure, driven by wet soil and poor hygiene. Use drained beds, strict cleanliness and limit entry to the room. [S3,S4,S6]
- Remove and destroy affected corms at once; growers report using neem oil on affected corms. [S6]
- Rodents are a major open-field problem; reduce bunds and hiding places, and use traps. [S7]
- The Kashmir package suggests a fungicide dip before planting; this guide does not name or dose chemicals. Ask SKUAST-Kashmir or your KVK for an organic or biocontrol alternative. [S7]
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Check temperature and humidity daily with a logger; check corms weekly for soft, discoloured or mouldy patches. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Track sprouting date, flower initiation and flower count against your temperature schedule. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Flowers are picked by hand in the morning when open; stigmas are separated and dried below about 55 C. [S1,S3]
- Reported sale prices (several lakh rupees per kg, sometimes more) come from news and grower claims and are not verified; the quantity from a small room is small. Do your own numbers with a real buyer. [S9,S5]

### Risks and costs

- Capital is high (cooling, humidity, lights, electricity, corms) and failure from heat, power cuts or corm rot is costly. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Saffron has a premium market partly because of Kashmir's origin and certification; confirm how you would sell and what a buyer will accept. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Anyone starting should first train or visit an operating unit; SKUAST-Kashmir and Telangana Horticultural University both run or advertise training. [S1,S2]

## Avocado: how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 4/5 · capital needed: high · risk: high · first return: grafted plants bear earlier than seedlings; seedlings can take about 6 years (one source)

*Good money in the right place (mild, frost-free hills of the south and west, and the north-east hills) but slow to pay back, needs two compatible flowering types, and fails in hot dry or frost-prone areas.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **frost** (fixable): It withstands a mild winter but cannot tolerate freezing; fruit is very sensitive to freezing temperature. Do not plant where hard frost occurs; in mildly cool sites choose a sheltered slope. [S4,S5]
- **heat** (fixable): It cannot tolerate very high temperatures or hot dry winds. In hot dry plains this is a poor bet even with irrigation; windbreaks and shade only partly help. [S6,S4]
- **dry** (fixable): Rainfall near 1,000-1,500 mm suits it. In lower rainfall give regular irrigation, especially at flowering, fruit set and fruit growth when it is sensitive to moisture stress. [S1,S5]
- **wet** (fixable): It cannot tolerate waterlogging. Plant on mounds or slopes with good drainage; avoid black (clay) soil. [S1,S7]
- **ph** (fixable): Aim for pH about 5-7 (5.5-6.5 ideal). It does not tolerate saline soil. [S1,S7]

### Planting material

- Use grafted plants of a variety suited to your zone. ICAR-IIHR (Chettalli) released Arka Supreme (A type) and Arka Coorg Ravi (B type) for Karnataka, Kerala and Tamil Nadu. [S1]
- Plant both A-type and B-type varieties whose flowering overlaps (1:1 or 2:1) for pollination. [S7]
- Buy from an ICAR-IIHR / KVK / state-approved nursery; avoid unverified grafts. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Land preparation and planting

- Dig 1 m x 1 m x 1 m pits in April-May and fill with farmyard manure and topsoil (1:1). Plant in June-July or September depending on rainfall. [S1]
- Grafts at about 6 m x 6 m (about 110 plants per acre); seedlings need wider spacing (8-10 m). [S1]
- It cannot tolerate shade or strong winds (brittle wood); give it full sun and a windbreak. [S6,S3]

### Irrigation

- Avoid waterlogging; use basin or drip irrigation and irrigate during flowering, fruit set and fruit growth. [S5,S7]
- Mulch the basin to conserve moisture. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Nutrition (organic)

- Compost and farmyard manure each year, increasing the amount as the tree grows; avoid heavy pruning, which pushes leaf growth over fruit. [S1,S6]

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Thrips: a thick (about 15 cm) mulch layer reduces pupae in the soil. [S6]
- Fruit flies: collect and destroy fallen infested fruit; use pheromone traps. [S6]
- Main diseases are anthracnose and scab; improve air flow, avoid wet canopies and remove infected fruit and twigs. [S6]
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Inspect new shoots, flowers and fruit every week or two for thrips, fruit-fly stings and dark spots. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Record flowering dates (September-November or February-March) and fruit set to learn your block. [S1]

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Fruit is harvested when mature but still hard and ripens off the tree; harvest by clipping with the stalk. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Yield depends heavily on variety, pollination and age; do not rely on blog yield figures, ask ICAR-IIHR Chettalli or your KVK for local numbers. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Risks and costs

- Payback is slow (several years) and the tree is the main capital item. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- In India it is concentrated in the southern and western hills; check proven local growers before planting at scale. [S8]

## Pomegranate: how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 4/5 · capital needed: medium · risk: high · first return: fruiting from about the 4th year (cited); useful crop from year 3-4

*Suits hot, dry, semi-arid land with drip irrigation, and it earns well. But bacterial blight has made many growers uproot orchards, so disease-free planting material and strict hygiene decide success.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **heat** (fixable): Pomegranate endures up to about 48 C with hot dry winds; fruit colour and quality are best in a hot dry fruiting season. Heat is rarely the limit; water is. [S1]
- **dry** (fixable): Water is the limiting factor in arid regions; use drip irrigation, mulch 15-20 cm thick, and plan the water budget before planting. [S1,S4,S5]
- **wet** (fixable): Humid, rainy, cloudy fruiting seasons raise disease (bacterial blight, fruit spots) and cracking. Prefer a hot dry fruiting season; choose your flowering season (bahar) to avoid heavy rain on fruit. [S6,S1]
- **frost** (fixable): It is deciduous and tolerates cold winters when irrigated, but young trees can be damaged by hard frost; protect them in the first winters. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- **ph** (fixable): Ideal pH is about 6.5-7.0, but good orchards in Solapur grow in alkaline soils (pH around 7.8-8.8). Add compost; avoid saline patches. [S1,S7]

### Planting material

- Use certified, disease-free, true-to-type plants free of nematodes, wilt and bacterial blight; tissue-cultured plants from approved sources are one option. ICAR-NRC on Pomegranate (Solapur) is the reference institute. [S1,S8]
- Infected planting material is how bacterial blight reaches new areas, so never buy cheap unverified saplings. [S8]
- Jalore Seedless and G-137 (bred for blight tolerance) are among varieties named, besides Bhagwa; ask your KVK what works locally. [S7,S9]

### Land preparation and planting

- Deep alluvial or sandy to sandy-loam soil is best. Plant at about 3 m x 3 m (about 1,111 plants per hectare for Bhagwa); some advice uses wider 4.5 m x 4.5 m to improve airflow. [S1,S6]
- Plant in the monsoon (June-August) or February-March. [S9]
- Mulch the root zone 15-20 cm thick with weeds and dry grass. [S1]

### Irrigation

- Drip irrigation is standard. A drip trial at ICAR-NRCP gave tree water use of about 5.7-13 litres per day in year 2 rising to 10-23 litres in year 5 for Mrig bahar. [S10]
- Crop regulation (bahar) uses planned water withholding, for example April-May for the Mrig bahar crop; learn the schedule from a trained source before trying it. [S11]

### Nutrition (organic)

- Farmyard manure or compost around the drip line each year, with mulch; this guide does not give chemical fertiliser doses. Use a soil test and your KVK's schedule. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Bacterial blight is the most damaging disease. Prevent with disease-free plants, wide spacing and airflow, and removing infected fruit and twigs and burying them in a covered pit. [S6,S12]
- Biological options studied include Pseudomonas fluorescens and Bacillus subtilis; ask your KVK for a registered biocontrol product and method. [S6]
- Use drip, not sprinklers, so the canopy stays dry; disinfect pruning tools between trees. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Institute advisories do list chemical sprays; this guide does not reproduce them. Get the current local schedule from ICAR-NRCP or your KVK. [S12]
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Walk the orchard weekly: look for dark leaf spots, oily fruit spots, cracking and wilting branches, and remove affected material immediately. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Keep a log of flowering, fruit set, irrigation and disease sightings per tree block. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Fruiting from about the 4th year; harvest timing depends on the bahar (for Mrig bahar, December-January at Solapur). [S13,S11]
- Improved management including blight control raised net returns about 25 percent over farmer practice in an Andhra Pradesh trial of 50 farmers. [S13]

### Risks and costs

- A reported 30 percent or more of farmers uprooted trees over four years because of blight, wilt and similar diseases. [S6]
- Water supply and a reliable market and grading are the other big risks; plan both before planting. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

## Turmeric: how to grow it

**For a first-timer:** difficulty 3/5 · capital needed: medium · risk: medium · first return: harvest about 6-9 months after planting (varieties range from about 188 to 285 days)

*Well-known crop with strong ICAR-IISR guidance and a ready market, but it needs a lot of seed rhizome, mulch, water and careful rot prevention, and the price swings.*

### If your conditions are off, here is what to do

- **dry** (fixable): Under about 1,500 mm of rain it needs irrigation: about 15-23 irrigations in clayey soils and about 40 in sandy loam. Confirm you have reliable water for the whole crop before planting. [S1,S3]
- **wet** (fixable): Good drainage is essential. Plant on raised beds (about 15-30 cm high) with drains between them; waterlogging causes rhizome rot. [S2,S3]
- **ph** (fixable): It tolerates a wide range (about 4.5-7.5). Add compost; on laterite soils lime is applied per local advice, so get a soil test. [S2,S4]
- **frost** (fixable): It is a tropical crop and is not frost tolerant; do not plant where winter frost occurs during growth. In cool sites delay planting until danger has passed. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- **heat** (fixable): Grown up to about 35 C as a normal range; mulch and irrigate more in heat. [S1]

### Planting material

- Use whole or split mother and finger rhizomes that are healthy and disease-free. About 2,000-2,500 kg of rhizome is needed for one hectare. [S2,S1]
- ICAR-IISR (Kozhikode) varieties include IISR Prabha, Prathibha, Kedaram and Alleppey Supreme; the IISR Experimental Farm, Peruvannamuzhi supplies planting material. [S1]
- Where nematodes occur use only healthy, nematode-free rhizomes. [S1]

### Land preparation and planting

- Prepare land with the first monsoon showers (fine tilth, four deep ploughings). Plant in April-May with pre-monsoon showers in Kerala and the west coast; other regions follow local advice. [S1,S4]
- Plant in pits about 25 x 30 cm on raised beds, or on ridges 45-60 cm apart with 25 cm between plants. [S1,S4]
- Spread 30-40 tonnes per hectare of farmyard manure or compost basally. [S1]
- Mulch immediately after planting with green leaves (about 12-15 tonnes per hectare) and repeat at about 40 and 90 days. [S1,S5]

### Irrigation

- Rainfed in high-rainfall areas; otherwise irrigate regularly (about 15-23 times in clay, about 40 in sandy loam). [S3]
- Keep beds drained between irrigations. [S2]

### Nutrition (organic)

- For organic production: farmyard manure (about 40 t/ha) with vermicompost (5-10 t/ha) and green-leaf mulch; neem cake at about 25 g per planting pit. [S5,S6]
- Pour cow-dung slurry over beds after each mulching to boost soil microbes. [S7]

### Pests and diseases (organic and cultural only)

- Rhizome rot is the major disease: use healthy rhizomes, raised drained beds, Trichoderma-enriched compost at planting, and treat seed rhizomes with Trichoderma viride or Pseudomonas. [S6,S8]
- Shoot borer is the most serious pest: scout regularly, cut open affected shoots, pick out the larvae and destroy them. [S1,S7]
- Nematodes: healthy seed and more soil organic matter help; the biocontrol fungus Pochonia chlamydosporia is used in beds (ask your KVK for a source). [S1]
- ICAR guidance includes chemical options; this guide does not reproduce them. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- For anything beyond these organic and cultural steps, ask the nearest Krishi Vigyan Kendra (KVK) or state agricultural university for their current recommendation. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)

### Monitoring

- Weekly: look at the collar of the plant for water-soaked softness (rot), leaf spots and blotches, and 'dead-heart' shoots (borer). (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
- Weed at about 60, 90 and 120 days after planting. [S4]

### Harvest, yield and post-harvest

- Harvest when leaves yellow and dry; ICAR-IISR varieties such as Prabha (about 37.5 t/ha) and Prathibha (about 39 t/ha) reported fresh rhizome yields in about 190-195 days. [S1]
- Cure by boiling the rhizomes in fresh water then sun-drying for about 10-15 days, or dry with hot air up to about 60 C. [S7]
- Store seed rhizomes heaped in shade or a well-ventilated place covered with turmeric leaves. [S7]

### Risks and costs

- Seed is the biggest input (about two tonnes per hectare), so a failed or rotted crop is costly. [S1]
- For certified organic sale the crop must be under organic management for about 18 months to 2 years, with a buffer zone from non-organic neighbours. [S6,S9]
- Price changes a lot season to season; confirm a buyer before a large planting. (general knowledge, not verified against a source)
